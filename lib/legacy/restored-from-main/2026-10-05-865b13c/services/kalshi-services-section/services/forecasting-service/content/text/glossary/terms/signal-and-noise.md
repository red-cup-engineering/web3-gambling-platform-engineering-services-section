**Signal and noise**, in [Nate Silver](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/biographies/nate-silver.md)'s usage, names the observation that in most data-rich forecasting domains — elections, weather, sports, seismology, economics — genuine predictive signal is mixed with far more noise than intuition suggests, and that much of the practice of forecasting consists of separating the two rather than simply accumulating more data.

Central to the framework is a sharp distinction between a **forecast** and a **prediction**: a forecast is a probability distribution over possible outcomes, updated continuously (in the Bayesian sense) as new evidence arrives, while a prediction is a single, deterministic claim about what will happen. Collapsing the distinction — treating a 70% probability as equivalent to a confident "yes" — is, on this account, the most common form of public forecasting failure, and it is the source of much of the criticism aimed at probabilistic models whenever a lower-probability outcome occurs.

Signal and noise shares its underlying discipline with [superforecasting](./superforecasting.md): both require a forecast to be stated as a probability, scored against outcomes, and revised as new evidence arrives, rather than judged solely on whether a single confident claim turned out to be right.
